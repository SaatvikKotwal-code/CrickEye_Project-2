\chapter{Literature Review}
\begin{justify}

This chapter surveys computer vision in sports, pose estimation, video action recognition, and cricket-specific work, and states the gaps CrickEye fills.

\section{Computer Vision in Sports Analytics}

\par Vision for sports has matured with deep learning and large video corpora. Thomas et al.\ (2017) survey production systems that still leaned on multi-camera rigs, controlled lighting, and heavy compute \cite{Thomas2017}; single-camera coaching deployments remain rarer.

\par Monocular racket-sport work includes tennis stroke modelling (Kovalchik and Reid, 2018) and badminton shuttle tracking (Huang et al., 2019) \cite{Kovalchik2018,Huang2019}. Cricket adds short explosive strokes (often 20--40 frames), strong depth ambiguity from a front-on net view, and a small ball often hidden by the net.

\par Broadcast stacks (e.g.\ Hawk-Eye, 2001) use many synchronised cameras and proprietary hardware --- far beyond a typical Tier-2 academy budget \cite{HawkEye2001}.

\section{Human Pose Estimation}

\par Deep learning now drives 2D pose from RGB: OpenPose (Cao et al., 2017) \cite{Cao2017}, MediaPipe (Lugaresi et al., 2019) \cite{MediaPipe2019}, and YOLOv8-Pose (Jocher et al., 2023), which jointly detects people and regresses 17 COCO joints in one pass \cite{YOLOv8,Lin2014MicrosoftCOCO}.

\par CrickEye uses YOLOv8-Pose for speed, COCO-17 coverage, largest-bbox person selection, and shared Ultralytics tooling with ball detection.

\par Any single front-on view loses reliable depth; Z-dependent quantities (true weight transfer, sagittal knee angle, spine tilt to ground) are weak. The headline composite therefore uses in-plane cues plus optional execution from length; swing intensity and bat speed stay diagnostic, not core weights.

\section{Video-Based Action Recognition}

\par Video action recognition moved from dense trajectories (Wang and Schmid, 2013) through 3D CNNs (Tran et al., 2015; Carreira and Zisserman, 2017) toward models that treat space--time volumes as first-class inputs \cite{Wang2013, Tran2015, Carreira2017}.

\par Transformer variants now dominate many benchmarks. TimeSformer replaces 3D convolutions with joint space--time self-attention divided across axes \cite{Bertasius2021}. Multiscale Vision Transformers (MViT) build hierarchical pyramids without convolutional stems, trading depth for efficiency on long clips \cite{Fan2021MViT}. These lines differ in inductive bias from Video Swin, which applies shifted windows in a 3D lattice \cite{Liu2022Swin3D}, but all three sit in the same design space as CrickEye’s Swin3D-T head: spatio-temporal context from RGB only, no depth.

\par CrickEye classifies 16-frame clips (zone-aware sampling, 224$\times$224, CLAHE + normalisation) with a linear five-way head, a pragmatic choice for short net strokes where full attention over long histories would add latency on consumer GPUs.

\par Training data are manually clipped and labelled from public YouTube nets into five classes, avoiding proprietary corpora; labelling is discussed with the dataset in Section~3.5.2.

\section{Cricket-Specific Computational Analysis}

\par Cricket vision work is thinner than for football or tennis. Three strands matter here:

\subsection{Ball Tracking and Trajectory Analysis}

\par Broadcast Hawk-Eye uses many cameras and triangulation; latency budgets differ from an academy net where a single phone records one batter \cite{HawkEye2001}. Academic ball trackers typically assume stereo or calibrated broadcast geometry (Ren et al., 2009; Reno et al., 2018); nets violate those assumptions through netting occlusion, motion blur, and perspective distortion \cite{Ren2009, Reno2018}.

\par CrickEye therefore treats ball analytics as an optional post-pass: YOLO-based proposals plus motion gating, axis-based rescaling, curvature cues for bounce, and yorker--short bins---always surfaced with confidence text because monocular pace remains indicative rather than umpire-grade.

\subsection{Batting Technique Analysis}

\par Video-based shot understanding in cricket is still sparse compared to football or basketball. Semwal et al.\ (2018) detect shot events from broadcast-style video using deep features, segmenting when a batter plays a stroke \cite{Semwal2018}; their contribution is event discovery rather than per-shot biomechanical grading or academy-ready tooling.

\par Wickramaratne and Wijeratna (2020) move closer to coaching vocabulary by extracting OpenPose skeleton descriptors for stroke classification from batting footage \cite{Wickramaratne2020}. Their evaluation foregrounds side-on capture---still common in broadcast archives---and stops after assigning a stroke label: no head stability, footwork timing, or composite scoring maps appear in that pipeline.

\par Wearable inertial systems remain an orthogonal branch (not cited here as ``video'' literature): gloves or straps can yield accurate swing traces but contradict many academy realities (equipment logistics, hygiene, child safeguarding). CrickEye deliberately avoids wearables: YOLOv8 pose plus Swin3D-T labels feed explicit session aggregates (\texttt{session\_summary}) and coaching alerts that mirror how coaches scan metrics on the live dashboard \cite{YOLOv8,Liu2022Swin3D}.

\subsection{Coaching Support Systems}

\par Commercial cricket apps often catalogue clips manually or quote speeds without tying vision outputs to repeated technique cues. Research prototypes likewise seldom expose APIs for uploads, WebSocket progress, coach/player separation, and printable reports in one stack.

\par CrickEye’s separation---analysis worker vs.\ FastAPI/WebSocket shell vs.\ Supabase roles---matches how Tier-2 centres operate in practice: one analyst laptop handles ingestion while athletes review phones.


\section{Research Gap}

\par The literature review reveals several gaps that CrickEye addresses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cost and accessibility:} Existing automated cricket analysis tools either require expensive multi-camera setups or wearable sensors, making them inaccessible to small-city academies.
    \item \textbf{Front-on camera focus:} Most prior work uses side-on or broadcast camera angles; few systems are specifically designed and tuned for the front-on net-practice view.
    \item \textbf{Integrated pipeline:} No existing open-source system combines pose estimation, video-based shot classification, biomechanical scoring, ball tracking, and a coaching dashboard in a single, deployable application.
    \item \textbf{Coaching language:} Academic systems typically output raw metrics or classifications without translating them into actionable coaching cues, drills, and session-level trend analysis.
    \item \textbf{Dataset from public sources:} Few cricket-specific classifiers document their training methodology using freely available YouTube footage, limiting reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

\par CrickEye is positioned to close these gaps for resource-constrained academies.
